Opening

2

Where we stand

Topic 3 of 13, week 3 of 14 – the third core conflict, and the first instrument you will be graded with. Behind you: evidence (T01) and the pattern vocabulary (T02); five prepared annoyance cases, plus whatever the Padlet for Topic 3 collected, are today's raw material. Today: the case, the formula and a ranking fight in teams of three or four; then what organizations do with wrong priorities, and you build the rubric yourselves. Next exercise: U2, after Topic 4, runs on the rubric you build today. Learning objective LO 3: order findings by severity, Bloom level 4, rising to 5 in the rubric build.

What you should already know. Severity in usability engineering classically combines impact, frequency, and persistence; Nielsen's 0–4 severity ratings are the best-known scheme. A finding is a described problem, ideally with location, evidence, and the affected task. WCAG (Web Content Accessibility Guidelines) defines testable accessibility criteria; "below WCAG contrast" means a measured contrast ratio under the threshold. App-store version ratings (as opposed to cumulative ratings) reflect reactions to a specific release, which is what makes them usable as natural experiments. All of this is look-up knowledge that any model explains well, so the session spends no time on it.

3 principle

The redesign wanted to do everything right and cost 1.7 stars: the most severe flaw bothers nobody in the review.

Severity lives in repetition: in the second tap that was not there yesterday, multiplied by three meals a day and by everyone whose routine just got heavier. MyFitnessPal passed its release through the usual checkpoints, and the damage turned up afterwards, in the version rating. This session builds two instruments that could have caught it earlier. In the first half you get a severity formula and stress-test it until it needs a footnote. In the second half the group builds a rubric, because part of the problem sits in the organization that shipped the release.

6

The reveal

MyFitnessPal, April 2026, version 26.16.0: the "Diary" tab became a card-based "Today" screen.

  • The release had no crash, lost no data and added no paywall
  • Every classical QA check would have passed it as a clean release
  • It even looked more modern, and no screenshot shows the damage
  • The redesign replaced the Diary tab, the screen where users log every meal
  • The version rating fell from 3.24 to 1.54 stars (MWM, April 2026)
7

What actually changed

Diary (before)

  • Logging food – the core task: fast
  • Copy meal: one tap, the daily tool of heavy users

Today (after)

  • Logging food: more taps than before
  • Copy meal: removed

Two changes carried the damage: the core task of logging food needed more taps than before, and the copy-meal function, a daily tool for heavy users, disappeared. Occasional users hardly noticed. People who log three times a day paid the extra taps at every meal, with no way around them.

8 principle

The release passed QA, and logging a meal got slower.

Crash reports, error logs and the QA checklist all stayed clean for version 26.16.0. The bug tracker had no ticket for the extra taps.

What severity is

9 principle

Severity = task criticality × frequency × inescapability

Ugliness and the volume of complaints stay out of the formula.

The factors multiply, so if any of them is zero, severity is zero. The two things left out matter as much as the three put in. Power users write loud reviews about trifles, while casual users uninstall without a word, so the volume of complaints tells you little about severity. Ugliness stays out for the reason the first factor gives: a flaw outside any real task costs nobody anything.

10

Factor 1 – task criticality

Does it hit the task the app exists for?

  • MyFitnessPal exists for logging, and the redesign hit logging
  • Answer it with what the app is actually used for, which the usage data knows better than the pitch deck
  • Teams like to grade the screens they are proud of, so criticality is the most overlooked factor
  • A flaw outside any real task multiplies out to zero, however ugly it looks

Of the three factors, task criticality gets skipped most often, and the reason is uncomfortable: answering it means admitting what your app is actually used for, and the usage data usually tells a plainer story than the pitch deck. MyFitnessPal is used for logging, so everything that slows down logging hits the app's reason to exist.

11

Factor 2 – frequency

How often does someone run into it?

  • Of the three factors, frequency is the strongest lever: it multiplies everything else
  • One extra second × three times daily × three hundred days a year = fifteen minutes per user
  • The same second in a settings screen opened once a month would have gone unnoticed
  • The numbers for this factor sit in analytics and support tickets

At three logs a day over three hundred days, a single extra second adds up to fifteen minutes a year per user, spread over nine hundred small interruptions. A settings screen that someone opens once a month could carry the same second without anyone noticing. The design team rarely holds the numbers to check this; they sit in analytics dashboards and in the support inbox.

12

Factor 3 – inescapability

Is there a reasonable workaround?

  • It often decides between annoyance and catastrophe: with an escape, a flaw becomes a detour
  • After the redesign, the only workaround for the copy function was re-assembling every meal by hand
  • A detour that doubles the task does not count as reasonable
  • Users who invent their own detours leave trampled paths in the usage data

Heavy users who had copied yesterday's breakfast with one tap now had to put it together again by hand, every morning. The product team would hardly have called that a reasonable workaround, and yet it was the only one the redesign left. With no escape, the flaw is paid in full at every meal.

Two error screens, one with an escape route

"Try again" is no escape route when the cause stays.

  • Wise shows a large exclamation mark and "Try again" twice, and says nothing about the cause
  • If the cause persists, the retry loop has no exit: inescapability in its purest form
  • Chase UK says "OK, that didn't work", names likely obstacles (hats, sunglasses, scarves) and offers a second path, "Take photo manually"
  • With that button, the same failure costs a detour, and the task still gets done

Three logs a day turn one extra second into fifteen minutes a year, and that is where severity is measured.

Resolution: rankings side by side

Only the places where the rankings diverge get discussed.

  • Live: the photographed rankings side by side, agreements grayed out, divergences marked
  • Ask each group: which factor produced your order? And which one did you silently skip?

19

The footnote the formula needs

For those affected, frequency and inescapability are measured within the affected group.

  • For those affected, an unusable screen is one hundred percent frequency and zero percent escape
  • Population averaging erases minorities without anyone intending it, which makes it hard to catch
  • Accessibility findings are the standing test case: the WCAG contrast failure ranked low exactly where groups averaged
  • A severity scheme that averages here ranks exclusion as a minor issue

The prepared list contained a deliberate provocation: a contrast failure below WCAG thresholds in dark mode. Across the population its frequency is low; for those affected there is no way around it. Groups that averaged across the population ranked it low; groups that counted within the affected group ranked it high. None of the groups that put it at the bottom wanted to exclude anyone, and the averaging put it there all the same.

Why reviews and organizations miss it

20

Why reviews miss it

Whoever sits in the review does not do the core task three times a day.

  • Walkthroughs, critiques and stakeholder demos all sample once, and single passes cannot feel multiplication
  • An expert notices what stands out on a screen; a repeated extra tap only hurts after weeks of use
  • The Today screen went through every checkpoint MyFitnessPal had and shipped
  • The repair needs both: expert eyes plus routine evidence from analytics, support tickets, the trampled paths of real use

Whoever sits in the review judges the app for an afternoon, and one afternoon with a prototype never adds up to three hundred mornings of logging breakfast. A process catches the repeated extra tap only when expert eyes sit next to routine evidence: analytics, support tickets, the trampled-path data of real use. One more source costs little to build: a feedback mechanism inside the app, kept as simple as possible, reaches the people who log three times a day before they write a store review.

21

Case 1: Sonos – the canonical disaster

May 2024: the app rewrite ships without feature parity – alarms, queue management, accessibility features missing.

  • Alarms and queue management are daily-use functions of a speaker app
  • Sonos switches off the old app
  • The protest holds for months
  • January 2025: the CEO is gone

The Sonos case runs in three acts. In May 2024 a full app rewrite shipped before it could do what the old app did: alarms, queue management and accessibility features were missing, all of them tools people used daily. The protest held for months. In January 2025 the CEO departed. Sonos had designers, and it had reviews.

22

Case 2: Plex – the rollback that worked

2025/26: Plex redesigns its TV app, and users protest.

  • Same starting point as Sonos: a rebuilt interface and a protesting user base
  • The protest runs for months
  • Plex reverts the layout (Neowin, XDA)
  • The rollback costs Plex face, and the users stay

Plex is the counter-case. Its TV-app redesign met protest that lasted for months, and Plex reverted the layout. The company lost face and kept its users.

23

Two protests, two exits

24 principle

Plex could still go back to its old layout; Sonos had already switched off its old app.

Reversibility counts as a severity factor at the scale of an organization. It is one of the candidates for the rubric the group builds in the second activity.

25

The missing role

Sonos and MyFitnessPal had capable designers and working reviews, and neither had someone whose job was to defend the severity ranking against the launch date.

  • Somewhere in each organization the list existed, with the right findings correctly ranked
  • Then the calendar overruled the analysis: the list lost to the launch date
  • Many of you will hold that role in a product team
  • The second activity trains building and defending criteria
  • Against this reading: a strategic rebuild can be worth a rating crash. That question stays open today.

Somewhere in each organization a list with the right findings existed, and on the day it mattered the list met a launch date and lost. Holding a severity ranking against the calendar was in nobody's job description. Many of you will have that job, and the rubric you build next is the first piece of equipment for it.

31

Resolution: top 3 vs. gut feeling

Where rubric and gut diverge, we ask: which of the two errs – and how would we tell?

  • Live: the top 3 by rubric, next to the gut favorites marked on the wall at the session start
  • The discussion stays on the gaps between the two lists
  • If the rubric errs, that is a legitimate result: it lacks a criterion, and we just found it
  • If the gut errs, the rubric did its job and overruled a mood
32

The moment the rubric fails

Every case where the rubric did not grip points at a criterion it still lacks.

  • Live: the noted cases from all groups
  • They go on the rubric's development backlog
  • Apply, collect the breaking points, sharpen: one full cycle ran today in 22 minutes
  • The backlog goes to Moodle with the rubric; if exercise U2 hits the same breaking points, say so in the strain line

Mature teams work with their scoring systems in this cycle: apply, collect the breaking points, sharpen. A rubric that has not broken yet has probably not met a hard case. The cases where today's rubric failed to grip are worth more than the smooth ones.

What this means for your project

Your SA analysis chapter names the three findings that hurt most and argues why.

  • It orders findings by the formula, footnote included, and by the rubric you just built
  • The grading criterion is prioritization
  • A model can list fourteen issues; the order of the top three and its reasons have to come from you
  • Write the ordering argument out, factor by factor

Severity – formula and rubric what counts for the exam today

Reserve: a second opinion – NN/g severity ratings

Nielsen's 0–4 severity ratings are the best-known scheme – our formula is built against it.

  • 0 no problem · 1 cosmetic · 2 minor · 3 major · 4 catastrophe
  • Combines impact, frequency, persistence and averages across the population by default
  • Source: Nielsen, J. (1994, continuously updated). Severity Ratings for Usability Problems. nngroup.com

To think further

  • If severity lives in repetition – what is the minimum evidence a review would need to feel multiplication, and who in an organization owns collecting it?
  • A strategic redesign knowingly accepts a rating crash. Who should sign that decision, and in what document?
  • Is the affected-group footnote a special rule for accessibility, or the general form of which population-averaging is the error?
  • In your last team project, who defended the severity ranking against the deadline? If nobody did, what does that predict?

What you should already know: the fidelity ladder – sketch, wireframe, click dummy, coded prototype – and the classical trade-off (speed vs. realism) are Bachelor material. Fidelity has dimensions (visual, functional, content, interaction) that can be mixed: a visually rough but functionally deep prototype is a legitimate and often superior combination. A design question is the question a prototype is built to answer; if you cannot state it in one sentence, no artifact can answer it. Any model explains these basics well; the session assumes them.

3 principle

High fidelity has become free and therefore worthless as a signal of maturity

For two decades, teams read polish as progress, and with reason: fidelity cost time, and whoever spent that time had usually thought first. Generative tools changed the price. A coded, animated, plausible prototype now takes twenty minutes and a decent prompt, so polish has stopped telling anyone how much thinking went in. This topic puts a fidelity decision in its place, made before building, justified the way one picks a measuring instrument, and defended when others attack it.

The ladder and its two economies

7

Each rung answers different questions – and lies about different things

The ladder's rungs answer different questions and lie about different things: sketches lie about timing and gestures, click dummies about data and performance, code prototypes about the distance to production. Whoever does not know the lies of their rung believes their own test results too much.

8 principle

For two decades, forty hours in pixels meant somebody had thought first. The old economy

Expensive signals are honest signals. Fidelity cost time, and time was the only thing polish could prove.

The rule "looks finished = is far along" worked for as long as fidelity was expensive. Whoever invested 40 hours in pixels had usually thought beforehand, because throwing those hours away would have hurt.

These are the same people answering, so the distance between the two numbers is the result. NN/g's video on AI-assisted prototyping (22.09.2025) compresses that distance into three words: speed can mask flaws.

10 principle

Test participants do not criticize finished-looking things

High fidelity suppresses the feedback it claims to collect.

The counter-case: people who cannot read a sketch tend to reject the rough version outright.

Show a sketch and people say "isn't something missing?" Show gloss and they say "looks great." The effect is old; generative tools have made gloss the default. Roughness has its own price. An audience that cannot read a wireframe tends to reject the idea along with the drawing, and a stakeholder who needs to see a product may hand you nothing usable until it looks like one. So the artifact has to match the person you are asking, which is the decision rule applied to the audience.

11

What a prototype is for five jobs, and which of them polish serves

A prototype can do five jobs, and only one of them gets better with gloss.

  • Visualizing an idea puts it on the table in tangible form, and a paper sketch often does that job
  • User testing needs honest reactions, which the previous slide showed gloss suppressing
  • Troubleshooting is meant to surface flaws early, while "speed can mask flaws" (NN/g, 2025)
  • Faster iteration pays only while each variant stays cheap enough to throw away
  • Stakeholder persuasion is the one job polish serves, and a demo built for it cannot also count as a user test

These five jobs of prototyping (visualizing ideas, user testing, troubleshooting, faster iteration, stakeholder persuasion) are the classic list, and the free polish of generative tools pulls them apart. Four of them get worse as the artifact looks more finished: people hold back criticism, flaws hide behind the surface, and nobody likes to throw a finished-looking variant away. Only persuasion profits. So the first thing to write down about any prototype is which job it has, because the decision rule on the next slide only works once the question belongs to one job.

12 definition

The decision rule

First the question, then the cheapest rung that answers it.

Fidelity is a measuring instrument. Pick it the way you would choose between a thermometer and a barometer.

Cheap here means economical without losing insight. A coded prototype for a comprehension question looks like diligence and works as a measurement error, with suppressed feedback as its side effect. The rule forces the question into existence before the tool runs, and that is the step free polish tempts teams to skip. Nobody blames a thermometer for not measuring air pressure; prototypes deserve the same sobriety, and the same care in selection.

16

What the votes showed

The votes split where two rungs both "work", and then the argument is about price.

  • Live: the vote images of the four rounds, side by side
  • Almost every rung answers almost every question somehow, so feasibility was rarely disputed
  • The arguments were about what the cheaper rung stops showing
  • In round 1 the impertinent option tends to win: no prototype, just show the icon to five people

Across the four rounds, the arguments rarely turned on whether a rung can answer the question at all. They turned on what it costs and what the cheaper rung stops showing. Round 1 is the clearest case: the option without any prototype tends to win, because five people looking at an icon answer the comprehension question at almost no cost.

17 principle

Almost every rung answers your question somehow, so the work is turning down the ones that answer a different question.

19

Three kinds of questions

Sorting a design question by kind already does half of the rung choice.

  • Comprehension questions ask whether people understand it
  • Behavior questions ask whether people act as the design assumes
  • Value and trust questions ask whether people would pay for it and rely on it

Two case stories – one per direction of error

Case 1: the glossy prototype

  • harvested only politeness: "looks great", and no findings
  • the gloss muted exactly the feedback it was built to collect
  • told live, from project practice

Case 2: the paper prototype

  • could not answer a performance question, by construction: paper lies about timing
  • the test ran anyway and produced numbers that looked like evidence

Both teams had good prototypes. Both had picked the wrong instrument, one erring toward gloss, the other toward paper.

21 principle

The paper prototype in case 2 produced numbers about a performance question it could not represent.

The wrong instrument still delivers data: confident, citable, wrong. Nothing crashes and no participant complains, so the team keeps working with the numbers. A rung chosen by gut feeling gives nobody a handle to catch the error; a written justification gives colleagues something to attack.

27

Patterns from the fire

Most attacks land on one of three patterns, and the recommendation cards say how to repair each.

  • A solution in disguise: "how can we add feature X" already names the answer
  • A question no artifact can answer, such as trust over months or market success
  • Three questions in one, squeezed into a single rung

A disguised solution already names its feature, so any prototype of it can only confirm what was decided. Trust over months or market success needs evidence that no artifact delivers in a test session. And three questions in one force a single rung to answer all of them, although each would pick a different rung at a different price.

28 principle

A question that fell today has saved its project weeks.

Exposé, exercise and closing

29

The exposé, concretely

Your exposé is half a page with four elements and a declaration line.

  • The design question in one sentence, in its final version after the fire
  • The chosen rung of the fidelity ladder
  • The justification via the decision rule
  • The blind spot, named, together with what it will cost later
  • Due at the end of week 5, after Topic 5; the template is in Moodle

The fourth element carries the most weight. In the colloquium, defenses that name their own blind spot tend to hold, because nobody can spring it on them. The deadlines fit together: Exercise U2 produces this block, the exposé is due at the end of week 5, and the block goes into it unchanged. Half a page is the upper limit, and keeping to it is part of the task.

Reserve · Round 5b: five onboarding concepts – which one won?

Intuit tested five first-screen concepts for its SnapPayroll app. Vote before the answer.

  1. Direct immersion: straight into the app, with a "Take a quick tour" button
  2. Tour: a sequence of intro screens before "Get Started"
  3. Multi-step transparency: three dimmed overlays that explain the screen
  4. Single-step transparency: one dimmed overlay pointing to the first step
  5. Tip: one hint bubble next to the first action, the screen stays usable
37

Tour against tip how the five concepts did

Users skipped the tour and asked for the tip.

  • The tour screens went unread: people tapped "Get Started" at once, all four variants "equally poor"
  • Multi-step overlays drew "Do I have to read all this?"
  • The single overlay did better, yet raised "does this mean the app is too complicated for me?"
  • The tip won: context, options, and a clear first step, with the screen still in view
  • Source: Intuit SnapPayroll tests, via Neil (2014)

44

Sparring partner block for self-study, copy-ready

Deepening prompt 1 (refutation): "Refute as strongly as you can: 'High visual fidelity suppresses honest user feedback, so early prototypes should look deliberately unfinished.' Find the strongest evidence and cases where polish improved feedback quality."

Deepening prompt 2 (question sharpening): "Here is my design question: [paste]. Attack it three ways: show where it is a disguised solution, show what no prototype could answer about it, and propose the cheapest fidelity rung that answers the defensible core – with the blind spot I will have to admit in my exposé."

Check question: Ask the model which fidelity it recommends for your question – then ask it to argue the opposite rung with equal conviction. If both sound equally strong, that is uniform confidence with variable reliability (Topic 1) in decision form; the corrective is the decision rule.

Warning: Models love the middle of the ladder – they over-recommend click dummies. They also invent usability statistics about fidelity effects ("34 % more feedback on low-fi") that do not exist. Any effect claim needs an opened study; any rung recommendation has to start from your question.

To think further

Opening

2

Where we stand in the semester

Topic 1 of 13, week 1 of 14. This session is about checking a market claim before you build on it. The plan: two numbers, the rules of this course, the source chain, and two activities, first labeling claims by hand, then dissecting an AI market analysis. Exercise U1 follows after Topic 2 and looks at one app twice, at its claims and at its patterns. The milestones ahead are the exposé in week 5, the interim state in week 10 and the colloquium in the exam period. Every topic hangs one conflict sentence on the wall, and in the last session we sort all thirteen: settled, moved, or more open than before.

Formally you need no prior knowledge for the first topic. It helps to know what an app store is, that analytics firms sell market data, and that a claim and its evidence are different things. The semester will come back to that last point more than once. Market facts and the mechanics of push notifications get no classroom time in this course, because a language model delivers both more currently than any slide. Checking what the model delivers is part of the work here.

3 principle

The most-cited statistic of this field is seven years old – and nobody notices, because it sounds plausible.

The semester opens with a number, before any syllabus. Almost every decision you will make in this course, what to build, for whom, at which fidelity, rests on claims about markets and people. Some of those claims were measured. Others were copied from a copy, and a few come straight out of the tools you will use daily. A prototype built on the 80-apps number starts from a 2019 measurement that, as far as we could find, nobody has repeated.

How this course works

7

The exam

The seminar paper records your project, and the colloquium asks about the decisions behind it.

  • A seminar paper of 3,000–6,000 words documents what you built and why
  • A presentation of 30 minutes ends in a discussion three follow-up questions deep
  • Exposé in week 5, interim state with its discarded alternatives in week 10, then the colloquium
  • The colloquium questions come from a published pool; each topic's closing slide adds to it
  • Bonus track, graded vs. formation-relevant objectives, project timeline: all in the T00 deck in Moodle

Language models write fluent text, so a fluent paper proves little by itself, and the paper becomes the record of your project. The grade rests on the exposé, the interim state with its discarded alternatives, and the colloquium. Three follow-up questions about your own decisions are easy to answer if you made those decisions yourself. The questions come from a pool that grows with each topic and stays the same until the colloquium.

The organization deck T00 in Moodle carries the rest of the rules in full. Six of the nine learning objectives are exam-relevant; notification strategy, offline UX and dark patterns are taught seriously and never examined, and every closing slide says which is which. The five exercise double sessions U1–U5 make the bonus: active participation plus an upload within 48 hours, compensating up to 5 % of the presentation points (APO §25(2)); U6 is the lecturer's backup date. Your project runs from a design question in Topic 4 to the exposé in week 5, the interim state in week 10 and the colloquium in the exam period.

The source chain

9

The anatomy of a source chain

A market number typically passes three stations: the primary source that measured (with year, method, population), the secondary use that repackaged it, and the aggregator that ranks on Google and strips whatever context remained. Every station can work in good faith, and the number still loses its year on the way.

10 principle

Ask a model for app statistics and it hands you the aggregators that rank on Google. the fourth station of the chain

Since language models entered the loop, there is a fourth station: models cite the aggregators, decks cite the models, and the number gains authority at every hop while its evidence stays frozen in 2019. Today's first AI role, "excluded", follows from this: you judge claims by hand first, so that you recognize a broken chain when the machine hands you one. The second activity tests whether that works.

11

The timeline

Source hunts on the 80-apps number typically end in one of three ways: the chain reaches 2019 and stops, it ends at an aggregator page dated this year, or it circles, A cites B cites C cites A. In all three, what gets published as a 2026 statistic rests on 2019 data. Exercise U1 sends you on this hunt yourself, for the claims around one app.

12 principle

Sensor Tower sells market intelligence, so ask what its numbers earn for the people who measured them.

Sensor Tower sells market intelligence, aggregators sell clicks, platforms sell their own narrative. A commercial interest tells you which questions to ask a number: who paid for the measurement, and who profits if you believe it. An aggregator page typically takes over Sensor Tower numbers without a year and mixes them with numbers whose origin nobody can name, and the reader cannot see where one kind ends and the other begins.

This slide opened the market part of this course last year. The first line has no year, and no line names a measurer. 15

The claims

All four cards quote real, findable texts from app pitches and blog posts.

  • "Users abandon apps after one bad session"
  • "Push notifications increase retention by 3–10×"
  • "The average user checks their phone 96 times a day"
  • "Super apps are the future of mobile"
16

Labeling – 10 minutes

One claim per group: supported, supportable, or unsupportable – plus your criterion.

  • Teams of three or four · verdict + criterion, one sentence each
  • Supported: the source is found and checked. Supportable: a source probably exists and could be found.
  • Unsupportable means the chain dead-ends or circles, as on the timeline
  • The interesting fight is the line between supportable and unsupportable
  • AI role: excluded
17

Verdicts

Every group reports; with 15 to 20 people, every result gets heard.

  • Follow-up for each group: what would have flipped your verdict?
  • A criterion that nothing could flip does not count
18

What a good criterion looks like

Named measurer + named year + named population – or it's a vibe.

  • Who measured, and what do they sell? That is the measurer
  • The year means the year of measurement, whatever date the republication carries
  • "US smartphone users, 2019" is a narrower population than "people"
  • A fourth label, "plausible", does not exist here: the 80-apps number has sounded plausible for seven years

A claim is supported when measurer, year, and population are named and check out; supportable when a plausible source exists and could be reached; unsupportable when the chain dead-ends or circles. A chain can dead-end at any of the three questions, and the second one, the year, is where recycled numbers usually give themselves away.

19 principle

Ten minutes of checking just separated four claims that all sounded equally plausible.

Activity 2 · The autopsy

20 exercise

Autopsy of a prepared AI market analysis Activity 2 · 30 minutes

Task: dissect "Overview of the mobile app market 2026" along four quadrants: correct / plausible-but-wrong / missing / hidden assumption

Time: 18 min work + 9 min comparison · Form: teams of three or four, one quadrant sheet per team

Result: filled sheet – at least one entry per quadrant

AI role: object of examination. You just labeled claims by hand; now a model's text goes under the knife.

The specimen is a one-page text titled "Overview of the mobile app market 2026", generated before the session. Each group sorts its statements into four quadrants. The fourth, hidden assumptions, is the hardest to fill, because nothing in the text points to them.

23

Autopsy – 18 minutes

Four quadrants: correct / plausible-but-wrong / missing / hidden assumption.

  • Teams of three or four · at least one entry per quadrant
  • The hardest field is the fourth: what does the text assume without stating it?
  • AI role: object of examination
24

The quadrants, compared

Groups usually agree on "correct" and argue over "plausible-but-wrong".

  • The "correct" quadrant is confirmed quickly and teaches least
  • "Plausible-but-wrong" carries the exercise
  • If one group says "correct" and another "wrong", we open the source together
  • Typical fourth-quadrant finds: a US-centric population, pre-2023 category definitions, growth framed as universal
25 question

Did the analysis reproduce the 80-apps number? checked in advance – both findings count

If it did: there is the loop, live. If not: better training data, luck – or a patched famous trap? Single cases prove no reliability either way.

26

What the machine cannot do here

The confidence: uniform

  • Fluent, self-assured, well structured, always
  • Human tone at least weakly tracks certainty; the model's tone tracks nothing

The reliability: variable

  • It cannot tell you which of its numbers deserve trust
  • What is left to check is the output itself

A language model phrases every answer with the same high certainty, while the reliability of what it says varies from sentence to sentence. People misjudge their own certainty too; still, a hesitant colleague usually sounds hesitant. The model sends no such signal. Topic 5 has you generate UIs, Topic 6 turns that check into a workshop for them, and Topic 13 puts "agents replace apps" on the same table.

27 principle

The model writes every sentence with the same confidence, and its tone never tells you which numbers hold.

Closing

28 principle

Every market claim carries a source and a year – or is labeled as an assumption. Your seminar paper, rule one – verbatim from the grading anchors

Label an assumption as an assumption, and the grading anchors accept it.

From Topic 4 on, you decide what to build, and that decision stands on assumptions about markets and people. Before you invest weeks in one of them, run today's check on it: measurer, year, population. In your seminar paper this becomes rule one, quoted verbatim in the grading anchors. No master's project gets by without assumptions, and the anchors ask whether each one carries its label.

29

What counts for the exam today

Exam-relevant today (LO 5) – same heading as the script section.

  • How a source chain is built: primary → secondary → aggregator → model
  • The three-part criterion (measurer, year, population) and the three labels: supported / supportable / unsupportable
  • Uniform confidence vs. variable reliability, and a flip condition for every verdict
  • Colloquium question, verbatim in the pool: "Which of your market claims is the shakiest?"
  • Question pool, verbatim: "One paragraph of an AI market analysis – which two claims are wrong, and how could you have known without research?"

In the room, groups first labeled real market claims as supported, supportable, or unsupportable, each with a criterion that could be flipped. Then they autopsied a prepared AI market analysis along four quadrants and checked disputed statements at the source. If you were absent: the quadrant sheet and the specimen are in Moodle. Do the autopsy on your own before Exercise U1; without a group to argue with, it is harder.

39

Sparring partner block for self-study, copy-ready

Deepening prompt 1 (refutation): "Refute as strongly as you can: 'A market claim without a named measurer, year, and population is useless for design decisions.' Find the strongest real cases where acting on unsourced numbers was rational."

Deepening prompt 2 (chain hunt): "I will give you a market statistic. Trace its likely source chain: primary measurer, year of measurement, and the path by which it reaches 2026 publications. Then tell me your confidence in each link – and what you cannot verify from your training data. Statistic: [paste one from your field]."

Check question: ask a model for "current statistics on how many apps people use per day, with sources" – count the aggregators among the offers, and whether any named primary source postdates 2019.

Warning: in this field, models reliably attach current years to old data, cite aggregator pages as if they were measurements, and invent plausible report titles. Every report title needs an opened URL; every number needs the three-part criterion.

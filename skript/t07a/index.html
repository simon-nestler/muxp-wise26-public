3 principle

The same company that defends friction as protection ran an install flow that 65 % of users abandoned, until one simplification cut the drop-offs by 60 %.

In 2025 Apple simplified its install flow for alternative app stores in the EU, and Epic reported that 65 % of users attempting to install its store had abandoned the old flow, against 25 % after iOS 18.6: drop-offs fell by 60 % (TechCrunch, 01.10.2025). For every tap, this topic asks two questions: whom does it serve, and is its cost proportional to what is at stake?

What friction is

8 definition

Friction

Every interaction between intent and outcome – every tap, every field, every confirmation, every wait.

  • Friction is inherently expensive, and expensive things need a reason
  • Each step loses a fraction of users, and the small per-step losses compound down the funnel
  • The industry's reflex is to remove steps. This topic accounts for each one: whom does it serve, at what stake?

In the working definition, friction is anything between what a person wants and what they get: taps, fields, confirmations, waits. The definition leaves good and bad out. If friction were bad by nature, every deletion confirmation would be a defect, and the second half of this session defends some of them. Calling it expensive turns each interaction into a cost that has to be justified.

9

Where friction hides

Friction hides in four pattern families you know from Topic 2.

  • In forms, every field is a question, and questions cost
  • In the checkout, per-step losses compound and each step is a moment of doubt
  • Onboarding offers a goodbye option on every screen, before the user has seen any value
  • Every permission dialog is a moment of distrust (Topic 8 returns to it)

In Topic 2 forms, checkout, onboarding and permission dialogs were pattern families, described by what they do. This topic reads the same families by what they cost: each field, step, screen and dialog is a place where a user can drop out.

10

The three sources of friction check in this order

The order guards against cynicism and naïveté at once. Assume value first, interest second, neglect last – and demand evidence at each step.

Every friction point has one of three sources, checked in this order: user value (security, error prevention), organizational interest (data hunger, newsletter checkboxes, account walls), or nobody thought about it. Checking value first keeps you from cynicism, and demanding evidence at each step keeps you from naïveté.

11 principle

Friction that serves nobody still costs the user time, and the organization pays for it in trust.

15

Resolution: where the labels diverge

We compare only where the labels differ. There, almost always, sits the same discovery: hidden organizational interest.

  • The usual suspects: the pre-ticked box · the account wall · the address field the payment provider never needed
  • Almost all of them wore the label "negotiable"
  • Somewhere a conversion team and a data team share this checkout as their battlefield
  • The user walks across that battlefield, tap by tap

Each group walked the same real checkout on their own devices, wrote down every interaction and gave it one of three labels. Only the disagreements were compared, and they pointed the same way almost every time. Where one group wrote "necessary" and another "negotiable", the interaction usually served the organization: a box ticked in advance, an account demanded before payment, an address the payment provider never needed. It takes no bad intent. A team with a conversion target and a team with a data target each add their step, and the person buying has to get past both.

Every interaction must serve someone, or the organization books it as trust debt.

The pre-ticked box, the account wall, the address field the payment provider never needed: all three served someone, only not the person tapping.

Friction as care

17 question

Whom does the friction protect? the sorting question for your "necessary" labels

The person themselves, their assets, third parties – or the organization?

Sort what your protocols labeled necessary, plus four usual suspects: deletion confirmation · transfer check · age gate · cooling-off period.

18

The protection map

The fourth direction habitually wears the first three's costume. The cookie wall calls itself privacy. But it is not always a costume: the age gate protects third parties and the operator from the law, the payment check protects the customer's money and the bank's exposure. The necessary labels from the live test are sorted onto the map live.

The frictions the live test labeled necessary, sorted by whom they protect: the user from themselves, their assets, third parties, and the organization from liability. That fourth direction habitually wears the first three's costume; the cookie wall calls itself privacy. Reading the fourth direction as a costume every time is the other error, though. An age gate exists because a law says so, and it still protects the minor. A payment confirmation lowers the bank's fraud losses, and it still saves the customer's money. Interests overlap more often than the map's four arrows suggest, and where they do, the friction is defensible from both ends. What is not defensible is friction that serves the organization only and borrows one of the other three names to say so.

20 principle

Whose interest a step serves decides whether it is defensible.

Kit makes you type DELETE before the account and the kid profiles go; Vivid starts a 9.90 €-a-month subscription with one tap.

A typed confirmation before an account is deleted costs several taps and protects the person. A one-tap purchase with a subscription as default costs none and harms them. Count taps, and you rank the two the wrong way round. Ask whose interest each interaction serves, and they sort themselves.

22

Four tools for protective friction

The four tools differ in when the friction arrives.

  • An undo window lets the action happen and offers the way back: the Topic-2 pattern, now with an ethic
  • A confirmation before a photo deletion annoys; the same check before the account goes protects
  • Cooling-off puts a pause between decision and payment
  • A speed bump slows the last step before something irreversible

Undo is the only one of the four that works after the fact. Confirmation, cooling-off and speed bump all act before the money moves or the data is gone, and they differ in how much they demand: a tap, a wait, a slower final step. A confirmation that appears before every trivial action trains people to tap through it, so its protective value depends on how rarely it shows up.

39

Reserve: onboarding as friction, with a test finding

In Intuit's tests, users did not read the tour screens. They tapped "Get Started".

  • No frontloading: give what is needed to start, in small pieces, at the moment it is needed
  • Guided (Headspace) or interactive (Cherrypick, several questions about meals, household and diet before the first recipe): label every screen necessary / negotiable / groundless
  • Every question before the first value is a goodbye option. Whom does it serve, the recommendation or the profile?
  • Skippable, and everything in the tour available again later

The finding comes from Intuit's tests of SnapPayroll, a mobile paycheck calculator, reported in Neil's pattern gallery. The team tried a direct start, tours, two kinds of transparent overlays and a single tip. Users skipped the tour screens and tapped "Get Started"; the four tour variants did "equally poor". The overlays irritated ("Do I have to read all this?"), and the single tip tested well. The old course drew three rules from this: no frontloading, tutorials always skippable, and everything in them available later. For this topic, onboarding is a flow like the checkout, and it gets the same three labels.

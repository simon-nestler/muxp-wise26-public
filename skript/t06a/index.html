Opening

2

Where we stand

Topic 6 of 13, week 6 of 14 – Anchor I, a workshop session: fifty of the ninety minutes are audit work. The specimens are machine-generated UIs, three prepared ones plus whatever you bring from Topic 5, and the threads of four topics converge here: source discipline (Topic 01) · vocabulary (Topic 02) · severity (Topic 03) · first second and third minute (Topic 05). First: the demonstration, a negotiated reading, one audit round against the grid and your three questions. Then a context rubric goes after what the checklist cannot see, tested in a second audit. Exercise U3 follows: iterate, audit in rotation, answer every finding. LO 4 and LO 3 at Bloom level 5.

What you should already know. WCAG basics (contrast ratios, touch-target sizes, labels) as checkable letters: the lab assumes you can run a contrast checker. The Topic 03 severity rubric as built by this group (versioned in Moodle). The audit vocabulary: a finding needs location, evidence, and an anchor (a grid line, an audit question or a rubric criterion); a specimen is the artifact under examination; inter-auditor agreement, two independent audits that agree, is our small-scale replication criterion. Duan et al. (CHI 2024) and its companion UICrit (UIST 2024) are the reading. Their study mechanics (LLMs generating design feedback, evaluated against human critiques) you know from the pre-session reading.

3 principle

The generated UI passes every first glance and fails the concrete context – and the flaw is found only by those who know what to look for.

NN/g's testing in October 2025 found generated designs that looked solid while failing information hierarchy, omitting necessary functions, and violating WCAG despite explicit prompt requirements. The "Generated Inaccessible" study (Web for All Conference, 2026) measured systematic WCAG violations across AI UI tools. Auditing output like that requires what four of the semester's first five topics built, which is why this session is an anchor: the source discipline of Topic 1, now applied to visual claims; the vocabulary of Topic 2, without which a flaw has no name; the severity instrument of Topic 3, which puts findings in order; and the first-second/third-minute frame of Topic 5. In the second half the grid itself comes under examination. A second checklist would inherit its blind spots, so the groups write down knowledge about users and situations in a form that someone else can check.

The grid and the rules

10

The audit grid (NN/g, Oct. 2025)

The grid has four lines, one for each failure class NN/g documented in October 2025.

  • Line 1 asks whether the visual order matches the task order: information hierarchy
  • Line 2 looks for missing functions the task needs
  • Line 3 measures WCAG contrast, touch targets and labels with a checker
  • Line 4 checks consistency: does screen 3 keep screen 1's promises?
  • Four lines fit into one twenty-minute round

NN/g (Wang, Brown, Zhang, 24.10.2025) tested AI tools on real design scenarios, and the four lines of the grid are the failure classes they documented. Information hierarchy asks whether the visual order matches the order of the task. Missing functions asks what the task needs and the screen lacks, and it is the hardest line, because absence does not show on a screenshot. WCAG gets measured with a contrast checker. Consistency asks whether screen 3 still keeps screen 1's promises. The grid stops at four lines because an auditor in a twenty-minute round works from memory, and a list of forty would stay in the folder.

11

Audit rules

Four rules keep the audit on the artifact.

  • Nobody audits their own UI: strangers audit strangers, by assigned rotation
  • A finding without an anchor (grid line, audit question or rubric criterion) does not count, however right it feels
  • Findings are ranked with the Topic 03 severity rubric this group built
  • "The artifact is on trial, never the person." This sentence stays on the wall all session

Two of the rules protect the audit from sliding into taste. A finding needs an anchor, a grid line, an audit question or a rubric criterion, or it does not count, however right it feels. And findings are ranked with the severity rubric this group built in Topic 3, so a missing empty state does not end up on the same line as an icon somebody dislikes. The other two rules are for the authors, whose work is on the table. The rotation is assigned, and nobody audits their own UI. The artifact is on trial, never the person; that sentence stays on the wall all session.

Context knowledge

15

An Ingolstadt address which grid line catches this form?

No grid line catches it. The form is ordered, consistent and labeled, and it expects a US address.

  • State is a required field, and a German postal address has none
  • The postcode field is called ZIP code
  • Every field gets a green check for Menlo Park
  • The finding needs a side constraint: "our users live outside the US"

Screen: Vrbo checkout via Mobbin, ca. 2023

The Vrbo checkout asks for street, country, city, ZIP code and a state. Every field has a label, every value gets a green check. For a US address nothing is wrong. With a German address the user meets a required State field that the postal address does not contain, a postcode field called ZIP code, and nobody knows whether the form will accept the order. None of the four grid lines finds this; it takes the side constraint "our users live outside the US". Generated and copied forms inherit this structure, which makes it a good first criterion for a context rubric.

16

What context knowledge means

Context knowledge comes in three kinds, and none of them fits into a grid line.

  • Knowledge about real people: who uses the app, when, under what pressure. Kit personas do not count
  • The failure moment: rain, gloves, stress, one hand, bad network
  • Side constraints from accessibility beyond the checkpoints, law and the organization
  • In NN/g's testing (Oct. 2025) the tools met WCAG letters and missed situations; some broke WCAG despite explicit prompt requirements

Three kinds of knowledge count as context. First, real people: who actually uses the app, at what time of day, under which pressure, none of which a persona from a UI kit tells you. Second, the failure moment, when rain, gloves, stress, a single free hand or a bad network change what a screen may demand. Third, side constraints: accessibility beyond the checkpoints, legal requirements, the organization the app lives in. NN/g's October 2025 testing shows why the grid cannot cover them. The tools satisfied WCAG letters and missed situations, and some violated WCAG even when the prompt demanded compliance.

17 principle

The four grid lines catch what fails often, and the expensive findings – a German address in a form that requires a US state – need an auditor who knows the users.

Building the rubric

18 exercise

Build the context rubric Activity 2 · 15 minutes

Task: Per group: three context criteria the NN/g grid does not cover – each made checkable ("recognizable by …"). Then hand your rubric to the neighboring group; their second audit is its grip test.

Time: 15 min · Form: teams of three or four

Result: a rubric with three checkable criteria, swapped

AI role: excluded. The criteria come from what you know about users and situations, and building them is the exercise.

Groups build three context criteria the grid does not cover, each made checkable ("recognizable by …"), then swap rubrics with the neighboring group. The swap is the test: in the second audit a stranger applies your criteria to a foreign UI, and what does not grip gets sharpened or struck, as with the Topic 03 severity criteria. Typical members of a merged rubric are one-handed viability, failure-moment honesty, empty-data states and interruptibility. The group rubrics become one workshop rubric, versioned in Moodle, and it joins the SA grading anchors beside the severity rubric.

23

Resolution: grid vs. context, side by side

The grid found

  • the classes the four lines are built to catch: hierarchy, contrast, consistency
  • two or three comparisons, presented by their auditors

The rubric found

  • the failure moment that is rare and costs everything
  • if our two lists look different from that, we discuss the difference

Same UI, new lens: a context rubric built by another group. The yield is the comparison, what the rubric found that the grid missed, and the other way round. If the room's lists do not match the expected division of labor, the group discusses the difference. On the grid side, missing functions is the class to watch. A prompt describes what a screen should contain, and the generator builds exactly that. The zero-results state and the way back from screen 3 were in nobody's prompt, so they are not there, and a twenty-second glance cannot notice what is absent. NN/g documented the same class in October 2025.

24 principle

Grid, rubric and a second pair of eyes together still miss findings, and those surface after the launch. the honest balance of the anchor

What the lab delivers: a procedure someone else can repeat, with its limits written down.

Put the grid, the rubric and a second pair of eyes together, and they still miss things. The missed ones usually surface after launch, when real users meet the app in situations no auditor listed. Many organizations ship without even a written procedure.

27 question

Can context knowledge be stored – in personas, design systems, prompts? what we did not settle today

Today's rubric is an attempt to write context down so that a stranger can check it. Whether that knowledge survives the trip into a persona, a design system or a prompt, or decays the moment it leaves the person who lived it, the session leaves open.

34

Duan et al. (CHI 2024) – core findings, fallback

Automated UI feedback can

  • produce plausible, well-formed critique at scale, at speed, without fatigue
  • cover breadth: many screens, many heuristics at once

It cannot

  • know your users, your context, your constraints
  • see what is missing, because absence is hard to critique from a screenshot
  • escape its training data: it repeats the criticism that was frequent there
35

WCAG quick-check card

Three checkable letters for the audit (WCAG 2.2).

  • Contrast text 4.5 : 1, large text 3 : 1 (SC 1.4.3) · non-text 3 : 1 (SC 1.4.11), measured with a checker
  • Touch targets at least 24 × 24 CSS px (SC 2.5.8) · platform guidance: 44 pt (Apple), 48 dp (Material)
  • Labels every control carries name, role, value (SC 4.1.2); a placeholder is not a label
  • Tappable? can you tell what is tappable without relying on color alone (SC 1.4.1)?

36

Reserve: the right size in the wrong place

A touch target can pass the size check and still sit out of the thumb's reach. Audit size and position, in the grip of actual use.

  • The WCAG card checks size: 24 × 24 CSS px, 44 pt at Apple, 48 dp in Material
  • Position is checked by hand: hold the phone in one hand and try the top tabs with your thumb
  • A target that needs the second hand or a change of grip is a finding, with the grip as evidence
  • In the failure moment (umbrella, bag, handrail) one hand is all the user has

The size letters of WCAG say nothing about where a target sits. A tab bar at the top of a tall phone can meet every size rule and still be out of reach for a thumb when the phone is held in one hand. The check is simple and cannot be done from a screenshot: hold the device the way the user will, and try. A finding needs the grip as evidence ("top-left tab unreachable one-handed, needs a change of grip"). It fits grid line 3 when the target is too small, and the context rubric when the target is big enough and in the wrong place.

37

Failure-moment card: gloves in the rain

Your user wears gloves and stands in the rain. One hand holds the umbrella.

  • Which touch targets survive? Which gestures?
  • What happens when the network drops mid-task, or when she is interrupted?
  • Which of your three criteria notices any of this? And what would one look like that does?
  • Derivable candidates: one-handed viability, target size under motion, failure-moment honesty, interruptibility
38

Reserve: states a screenshot does not show

Grid line 2 asks for missing functions. Five checks cover the places where they go missing most often.

  • Empty and loading: what does the screen show before there is data?
  • Error: does the message name the problem in plain words, suggest a solution and appear on the screen, not in a modal?
  • Success: does the user see that the transaction went through?
  • Offline: does the task survive a dropped network?
  • Help: can a stuck user get help on this screen, without leaving the task?

A generated UI shows the state its prompt described, usually the one with data in it. The audit has to ask for the others. The error criteria come from the old course material on feedback: plain language, the precise problem, a constructive solution, placed on the screen where the error happened. A modal dialog hides it, and a prevented error is better still. Contextual help belongs to the same list: help that is tied to the point where the user is stuck and needs no information up front.

Sparring partner block for self-study, copy-ready

Deepening prompt 1 (refutation): "Refute as strongly as you can: 'Automated UI critique inherits the blind spots of UI generation, so human context audits remain necessary.' Use the strongest capabilities of current multimodal models and the Duan et al. (CHI 2024) findings – argue the human audit is becoming redundant."

Deepening prompt 2 (self-audit rehearsal): "Audit this UI concept against these four lines: information hierarchy, missing functions, WCAG, consistency: [describe or paste your UI]. Then tell me which of your findings you are least confident in and why."

Check question: Give the model your context rubric and ask it to apply one criterion to your UI. If its finding could apply to any app of this category, it produced a frequent-class finding wearing context costume.

Warning: Models audit confidently and generically: they over-report hierarchy and contrast, under-report missing functions, never know your users, and cite WCAG success criteria by wrong numbers. Verify every automated finding before it goes on a sheet.
